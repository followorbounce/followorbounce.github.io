Physics · Waves

Waves

Drop a stone in water, pluck a string, shine a light, or shake the ground hard enough — and the same equation describes all of it: v = fλ, speed equals frequency times wavelength. What differs from case to case is what's doing the vibrating, whether it needs something to vibrate through, and which direction it moves relative to where the energy is going. This is a walk through sound, light, the ground under your feet, and — if it exists in any other form — whatever's left.

01 — One Idea, Many Disguises

Three ways to wiggle

A wave carries energy without carrying matter along with it (mostly — more on that later). But the material actually doing the moving can move three different ways relative to the wave's direction of travel:

Longitudinal — particles move back and forth along the direction of travel, alternately compressing and stretching the medium. Sound in air is longitudinal: it's a moving pattern of pressure squeezed together and pulled apart. Transverse — particles (or, for light, the electric and magnetic fields) move perpendicular to the direction of travel, like a shaken rope. Orbital — a hybrid seen at the surface of water, where particles trace small circles, moving forward at the crest and backward in the trough, so the wave travels but the water itself mostly stays put.

Longitudinal — sound. Motion parallel to travel; medium compresses & stretches.
Transverse — light, S-waves. Motion perpendicular to travel.
Orbital — water surface. Particles circle in place as the wave passes through.
02 — Sound: Waves You Feel as Pressure

A moving pattern of squeeze

Sound is air (or water, or steel) being alternately compressed and rarefied by something vibrating — a vocal cord, a speaker cone, a struck bell. It needs a medium: no air, no sound, which is why space is silent no matter how loud the explosion. Human hearing runs roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz; below that is infrasound, above it ultrasound[1]. Other animals draw the line elsewhere — elephants communicate in rumbles below 20 Hz that people can't consciously hear, while dogs, bats, and dolphins hear well up past 20 kHz, bats and dolphins using frequencies around 100 kHz for echolocation.

Log scale, 1 Hz – 200,000 Hz. Markers show roughly where a few species' hearing sits.

03 — Earth's Own Vibrations

How a shockwave mapped the inside of the planet

An earthquake sends out two kinds of body wave at once, and they disagree about liquids. P-waves ("primary") are longitudinal — a compression wave, exactly like sound, just moving through rock instead of air. S-waves ("secondary") are transverse — a shearing, side-to-side motion. P-waves arrive first because they're faster, and they can travel through anything compressible: solid, liquid, or gas. S-waves can't — a fluid has no shear strength to sustain that sideways motion, so they simply stop dead at any liquid boundary[2].

That difference is how anyone found out Earth's outer core is liquid without drilling anywhere near it: seismographs on the far side of the planet from a big earthquake pick up the P-waves that punched straight through the core, but the S-waves never show up at all. A wave that refuses to go somewhere turned out to be as informative as one that does.

P-wave

Longitudinal, ~8 km/s through the outer core. Passes through solid, liquid, and gas.

S-wave

Transverse, slower, needs rigidity. Vanishes at the liquid outer-core boundary — the evidence itself.

Water waves sit between the two: at the surface of the ocean, the orbital motion from Chapter 01 means the water is doing a bit of both, circling forward-and-up, then back-and-down, as the wave itself travels on without it.

04 — Light: Waves That Need Nothing to Travel Through

The one spectrum, cut seven ways

Light is a transverse wave too, but of something stranger than matter: an oscillating electric field generating a magnetic one, and back again, neither needing a medium at all — which is the only reason sunlight can cross 150 million km of vacuum to reach a leaf. Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all exactly the same phenomenon; the names are just human labels drawn across one continuous range of wavelengths, all moving at the same speed c in a vacuum[3].

Log scale of wavelength, 1 picometer – 1,000 m — 15 orders of magnitude in one strip. Visible light (400–700 nm) is genuinely that sliver in the middle[3].

Past ultraviolet, wavelength gets so small that physicists mostly stop using it and switch to photon energy in electron-volts instead — ultraviolet runs a few eV to ~100 eV, X-rays 100 eV to 100,000 eV, and anything above that is gamma[3]. Same spectrum, different ruler, chosen for whichever end of it is easiest to measure.

05 — And Otherwise, If It Exists: Matter Waves

Everything is a wave, if you look close enough

In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that it isn't just light that behaves like both a wave and a particle — anything with momentum does, including electrons, atoms, and (in principle, unmeasurably) you. The wavelength is λ = h/(mv): Planck's constant divided by momentum[4]. Plug in an electron moving at 1% of the speed of light and you get a wavelength around 0.24 nanometers — close to the spacing between atoms in a crystal lattice. That's not a coincidence you're meant to shrug off: a beam of electrons at that wavelength diffracts off a crystal exactly the way light diffracts off a grating, which is the entire operating principle behind an electron microscope. Shorter wavelength than visible light means finer resolution, all the way down to individual atoms.

Bigger, slower objects have de Broglie wavelengths absurdly smaller than anything that could ever be observed — a thrown baseball's wavelength is something like 10⁻³⁴ meters — which is the real answer to "why don't I see myself diffract when I walk through a doorway." The wave is there. It's just too small by a margin no instrument will ever close.
