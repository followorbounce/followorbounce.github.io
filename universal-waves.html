The Hidden Vibrations of Reality

Universal Waves

Everything that oscillates can generate a wave — and an enormous share of the interesting phenomena in the universe turn out to be exactly that: something vibrating, propagating through matter, a field, spacetime itself, or a probability distribution. This page is a map of the whole territory, from a plucked string to a black-hole merger.

Primary question: what kinds of waves exist in nature, and how are they related?

Thesis: a guitar string, a beam of light, a brain rhythm, and a ripple in spacetime are all instances of the same small set of mathematical ideas — just applied to different things doing the vibrating.

01 — What Is a Wave?

A Disturbance That Carries Energy Without Carrying Matter

A wave is a repeating disturbance that transports energy — and often information — from one place to another, usually without the medium itself making the trip. A shared vocabulary covers every wave in this entire page:

Oscillation

Movement that repeats back and forth around a resting point.

Periodicity

The pattern repeats at a fixed time interval, the period.

Frequency

How many repeats happen per second (Hz) — the inverse of the period.

Amplitude

How large the disturbance is — height for a water wave, pressure for sound, field strength for light.

Phase

Where in its cycle the wave currently is — critical for how two waves add together.

Wavelength

The physical distance between two repeats of the pattern.

Resonance

A system's tendency to oscillate with much larger amplitude at specific frequencies (Chapter 15).

03 — Mechanical Waves

Matter Vibrating Through Matter

Mechanical waves need a physical medium — air, water, rock, a string — because the wave is that medium's particles pushing on their neighbors. Two shapes cover almost everything in this family: longitudinal waves, where particles move parallel to the direction of travel (compression and rarefaction, like sound), and transverse waves, where particles move perpendicular to it (like a shaken rope, or the shear waves inside an earthquake).

04 — Sound and Music

Organized Vibration Becomes Music

Sound is the mechanical wave family's most elaborated member: a pressure wave that, filtered through harmonics, resonance, and standing waves in an instrument's body or a vocal tract, becomes notes, timbre, speech, and music. Human hearing turns roughly 20 Hz–20 kHz of exactly this kind of vibration into everything from a conversation to a symphony.

05 — Electromagnetic Waves

The Only Wave That Needs Nothing to Travel Through

An electromagnetic wave is a self-sustaining loop: a changing electric field generates a magnetic field, which generates an electric field, endlessly, propagating at the speed of light with no medium required at all. Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all the identical phenomenon, differing only in frequency — and visible light is a genuinely tiny sliver of that whole range.

06 — Light: The Visible Wave

The One Band Your Eyes Can Read

Color is just frequency: the human eye's visible window runs roughly 400–700 nanometers, and everything about color perception is your visual system decoding wavelength. Light also does everything waves do generically — reflection (bouncing off a boundary), refraction (bending when it changes medium and speed), diffraction (spreading around obstacles and through narrow openings), interference (adding constructively or destructively with itself or other light), and polarization (restricting oscillation to a single plane, which is exactly how polarized sunglasses and LCD screens work).

07 — Radio Waves

The Same Light, Just Slower

Radio sits at the long-wavelength, low-frequency end of the exact same electromagnetic spectrum as light — a HAM radio signal, a GPS satellite's transmission, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and radar are all electromagnetic waves, distinguished only by frequency and how that frequency is put to use.

08 — Plasma Waves

Waves in an Ionized Gas

A plasma — a gas hot or energetic enough that electrons separate from their atoms — supports its own distinct wave types, since charged particles respond to electric and magnetic fields in ways neutral gas molecules simply don't. The classic example is the Alfvén wave, where magnetic field lines behave almost like vibrating strings, dragging the ionized plasma along with them.

Solar wind

A continuous stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun's corona at hundreds of kilometers per second, carrying the Sun's magnetic field out through the solar system.

Ionosphere

A layer of Earth's upper atmosphere ionized by solar radiation — dense enough to reflect certain radio frequencies back toward the ground, which is exactly the mechanism behind HF skywave propagation in Radio Communications.

Aurora

Solar particles funneled by Earth's magnetic field into the upper atmosphere near the poles, colliding with oxygen and nitrogen and making them glow.

Fusion plasma

Reactors like tokamaks must actively manage plasma waves and instabilities in superheated, magnetically confined plasma to keep a fusion reaction stable.

All of this feeds directly into space weather: solar plasma activity disturbs the ionosphere and Earth's magnetic field, which can degrade GPS accuracy, disrupt HF radio, and stress power grids — a direct, practical link between plasma physics and everyday infrastructure.

09 — Matter Waves

Even Particles Have a Wavelength

Quantum mechanics erases the line between "particle" and "wave." Louis de Broglie proposed in 1924 that any object with momentum has an associated wavelength (λ = h/mv), and this isn't a metaphor — electrons genuinely diffract and interfere like light, which is the entire operating principle of an electron microscope. An atom's electrons don't orbit like tiny planets; they exist as wavefunctions, spread-out probability amplitudes describing where the electron is likely to be found, not a definite trajectory.

10 — Gravitational Waves

Ripples in Spacetime Itself

General relativity predicts that accelerating massive objects — most dramatically, two black holes or neutron stars spiraling into each other — should radiate ripples in the curvature of spacetime, stretching and squeezing space as they pass. LIGO's first direct detection, in 2015 (event GW150914, two merging black holes), confirmed this a full century after Einstein predicted it, and the signal's frequency swept upward right before the merger into a literal audible "chirp" once converted to sound[1].

11 — Chemical Waves

Reactions That Travel

Some chemical reactions don't just happen everywhere in a solution at once — they propagate as a moving front, or even oscillate in place. The best-known example is the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, a self-organizing chemical system that rhythmically shifts color as it runs, producing visible traveling wave patterns and concentric or spiral rings in a shallow dish, entirely from chemistry with no external clock driving it. Similar self-organizing wave behavior shapes patterns in crystal growth, where a growing front's instabilities produce the branching, repeating structure of a snowflake or a dendrite.

12 — Biological Waves

Life Runs on Rhythm

Biological systems are full of periodic phenomena, several of them literally called waves in the clinical literature: brain waves (EEG activity, conventionally grouped into named frequency bands — delta ~0.5–4 Hz during deep sleep, up through gamma above 30 Hz during focused activity), heart rhythms (a resting heart beats roughly 1–1.7 times per second), and circadian cycles (a roughly 24-hour internal oscillator present in nearly all life, synchronized to daylight). At the cellular level, neural oscillations — networks of neurons firing in loose synchrony — are believed to help coordinate activity across different brain regions, an active area of ongoing research rather than a fully settled picture.

13 — Information Waves

Communication Systems Are Controlled Waves

Every modern communication system works by deliberately shaping a wave to carry a message: radio and satellite links modulate an electromagnetic carrier (Chapter 07); fiber optic cables send information as pulses of light bouncing down a glass core; even ordinary electrical wiring sends signals as a voltage wave along a conductor. A computer network, underneath every abstraction layer, is ultimately just very precisely controlled vibration.

14 — Planetary and Cosmic Oscillations

The Universe as a System of Resonances

Zoom out far enough and the pattern repeats at planetary and stellar scale. Pulsars — rapidly rotating neutron stars — sweep a radio (or X-ray) beam across the sky with extreme regularity; the fastest confirmed, PSR J1748-2446ad, spins 716 times per second, a rate that happens to fall inside the range of human hearing[2]. The Sun's entire surface oscillates with a dominant ~5-minute period, a global acoustic "ringing" studied by helioseismology[3]. Other stars show their own characteristic vibration patterns (asteroseismology), and orbital resonance — where two orbiting bodies' periods lock into a simple ratio, echoing the same small-integer-ratio logic as a musical interval — shapes the structure of moon systems and asteroid belts throughout the solar system.

15 — Resonance: Nature's Amplifier

Push at the Right Rhythm, Get an Outsized Result

Every oscillating system has one or more natural frequencies it "wants" to vibrate at. Push it at any other frequency and not much happens; push it — even gently — right at a natural frequency, and the response can grow dramatically, because each push arrives in phase with the last and energy accumulates instead of canceling out. This single idea explains why bridges and buildings are engineered to avoid matching frequencies with wind or foot traffic, why a radio antenna is cut to a specific length, why blowing gently but rhythmically across a bottle produces a loud, specific note, and — at planetary scale — why orbital resonance (Chapter 14) is common rather than rare.

16 — The Universal Mathematics of Waves

One Equation, Every Scale

The same handful of relationships describe every wave family on this page — only the symbols' meaning changes.

v = f × λ
Wave speed = frequency × wavelength — true for sound, light, radio, and water waves alike.
T = 1 / f
Period and frequency are reciprocals: a 2 Hz wave has a 0.5-second period, whether it's a heartbeat or a light wave.
f = f₀ × 2^(n/12)
The equal-tempered harmonic relationship behind musical scales — see Music Is Mathematics.
λ = h / (mv)
De Broglie's matter-wave relationship — Planck's constant divided by momentum.

The recurrence isn't a coincidence or a loose metaphor: all of these are, mathematically, solutions to closely related differential equations describing oscillation and propagation. Learning the wave equation once means recognizing it everywhere — in a plucked string, a laser, a Wi-Fi signal, and a wavefunction.

Music, speech, radio, light, satellites, stars, quantum particles, and spacetime itself are not separate subjects that happen to share some vocabulary. They are the same phenomenon, expressed at wildly different scales, in wildly different media, put to wildly different uses — a plucked string and a pulsar are, underneath, doing the same thing.

Sound is a wave. Music is a wave. Light is a wave. Radio is a wave. Matter behaves like a wave. Spacetime can support waves. Information travels through waves. The universe, from a vibrating atom to a colliding pair of galaxies, is oscillating at every scale it has.
