Radio & Electronics
The Invisible Infrastructure

Radio Communications

Radio is not a single technology or a single frequency range. It is a vast electromagnetic communication medium used for everything from human speech and broadcasting to spacecraft telemetry, radar, navigation, remote control, military systems, and machine-to-machine communication — a phone, a weather balloon, a Mars rover, and a shipping container's tracker are all, underneath, doing the same physical thing.

Amateur ("ham") radio is one small, legally accessible window into this — not the thing itself.
01 — What Is Radio?

A Signal Is Not Automatically Information

Radio communication uses electromagnetic waves — the same phenomenon as light, just at frequencies our eyes don't see — to move information through space without wires. But a radio wave by itself doesn't contain voice, data, or "the internet." It's a carrier. Something has to impose the information onto it, and something has to know how to take it back off.

Ten stages, always in this order, whether the payload is a sentence or a spacecraft's core temperature.

Source is whatever originates the message — a voice, a sensor, a keyboard. Encoding turns it into a signal shape (analog voltage, or a string of bits). Modulation impresses that shape onto a radio-frequency carrier by varying its amplitude, frequency, or phase. The transmitter amplifies the result to useful power; the antenna converts it into an actual electromagnetic wave. Propagation is the wave's trip through space, air, or vacuum — and the part nobody fully controls. A receiving antenna catches a tiny fraction of that energy back, the receiver amplifies and filters it, demodulation reverses the modulation step, and what's left — if everything worked — is the original information.

02 — The Spectrum

One Continuous Range, Cut Into Named Boxes

The electromagnetic spectrum used for radio is physically continuous — there's no seam at 30 MHz. The ITU divides it into nine decade-wide bands purely for engineering and regulatory convenience, each one covering exactly a factor of ten in frequency[1]. Nothing physically changes at a boundary; a wave at 299 MHz and one at 301 MHz behave almost identically, they just fall on different sides of a filing-cabinet label.

03 — Frequency, Channels & Bandwidth

"145 MHz" Is a Point. A Channel Is a Range.

A radio doesn't transmit on an infinitely thin frequency — it occupies a band of frequencies clustered around a center (carrier) frequency. That width is the bandwidth. A voice channel might be 2.5 kHz wide; a Wi-Fi channel might be 20 or 40 MHz wide — both are "a frequency," but one carries a sentence and the other a video call.

Everything inside the bracket is "the channel." Everything outside it should, ideally, be silence.

Related but distinct terms: carrier frequency (the center point), symbol rate (how many discrete signal states are sent per second), and data rate (how many bits per second those symbols ultimately represent — which can exceed the symbol rate if each symbol encodes several bits at once). More bandwidth generally allows more information per second, but it also demands more spectrum, tighter engineering, and — almost always — a license or an allocation that permits it.

04 — AM vs FM

Two Ways to Hide a Signal Inside a Wave

A carrier wave has three properties you can vary: amplitude, frequency, and phase. The two oldest, simplest strategies each pick one.

AM — the message rides on the carrier's amplitude. The envelope (dotted) traces the message shape.
FM — amplitude stays constant; the message rides on how fast the carrier oscillates.

AM — Amplitude Modulation

Conceptually the simplest form of modulation, and historically the first. Still everywhere: AM broadcast radio, most HF communication, and — notably — civil aviation VHF voice. Aviation keeps AM largely because of how it fails: when two AM transmissions overlap, both are still audible (garbled together); with FM, the stronger one silences the weaker completely (the "capture effect"). For air traffic control, hearing that someone is transmitting — even garbled — beats being cleanly cut off by whoever keyed up first[2].

FM — Frequency Modulation

Keeps a constant amplitude "envelope," which makes it naturally resistant to the amplitude noise (static, electrical interference) that plagues AM. That's why it became the standard for VHF/UHF voice: land-mobile radios, amateur radio repeaters, broadcast FM, and many satellite voice links.

Neither is "better" in general — AM and FM are just the two most intuitive starting points. Real systems today mostly use richer digital schemes described next, chosen for spectral efficiency, robustness, or compatibility rather than either extreme.

05 — Digital Radio

Not "Analog vs. Digital Frequency" — a Different Question Entirely

Digital radio isn't a frequency range or a single technique — it's modulation applied to discrete symbols instead of a continuous message. The carrier still only has amplitude, frequency, and phase to work with; digital schemes just use combinations of those to represent bits.

Error correction is the quiet workhorse here: it lets a receiver reconstruct bits that arrived damaged, without asking for a retransmission. Combined with efficient symbol mapping, this is why digital radio can pack more reliable data into the same spectrum than an equivalent analog signal — and why it underlies modern networking, encryption/authentication (where legally applicable), and machine-to-machine communication.

06 — Propagation

Radio Waves Do Not All Travel the Same Way

How far and how reliably a signal travels depends heavily on its frequency and what's in its path.

Same planet, three different journeys, depending on frequency.

Ground wave hugs the Earth's curvature — effective at LF/MF, which is why AM broadcast can reach well past the horizon. Skywave bounces off the ionosphere and back down, letting HF signals sometimes cross oceans on a few watts. Line-of-sight dominates at VHF and up — if you can't "see" the other antenna (accounting for Earth's curve), the signal is weak or absent, which is why VHF/UHF radios often rely on repeaters on hills or towers. At microwave frequencies, links become more sensitive to physical obstacles, rain attenuation, and atmospheric conditions, while also offering far more usable bandwidth — the eternal trade in radio engineering. Diffraction, reflection, and scattering all still apply everywhere, bending and bouncing signals around obstacles to varying degrees; none of this is an absolute rule, just a set of tendencies that gets stronger as frequency rises.

07 — Spacecraft Communications

The Only Wire Long Enough to Reach Another Planet

A spacecraft's connection to Earth is, underneath everything else, the same ten-stage pipeline from Chapter 01 — just stretched across a distance where "line-of-sight" is measured in light-minutes.

TM (telemetry) travels down; TC (telecommand) travels up — watch the label switch as the packet changes direction.

Real spacecraft separate their radio traffic into distinct functions rather than sending one undifferentiated stream:

TM — Telemetry (spacecraft → ground)

Temperature, voltage, current, battery state, attitude, position, propulsion state, instrument measurements, system status — the spacecraft continuously reporting on itself.

TC — Telecommand (ground → spacecraft)

Change configuration, activate an instrument, change operating mode, upload parameters, execute a scheduled operation — the ground telling the spacecraft what to do.

Alongside TM/TC, missions also handle tracking, navigation, ranging (measuring distance from signal travel time), and separate science/payload data channels — usually with carefully engineered protocols (like CCSDS standards) rather than raw sensor values thrown onto the carrier.

Spacecraft frequency bands

"S-band" or "X-band" name a microwave region, not one frequency. NASA's Deep Space Network, for example, has used S-band (~2–4 GHz) since the earliest missions, added X-band (~8–12 GHz) for more data capacity, and now uses Ka-band (~27–40 GHz) for the highest rates[3] — with VHF and UHF still common for near-Earth missions like CubeSats. These are mission- and regulator-specific choices, not universal rules.

Lower frequency (e.g. VHF, S-band)

Needs a larger antenna for the same beam narrowness, often has more forgiving propagation, but offers limited bandwidth.

Higher frequency (e.g. X, Ku, Ka-band)

Can carry far more bandwidth through a smaller antenna, at the cost of a narrower beam that's less forgiving of pointing error and more sensitive to the atmosphere.

Why radio, specifically

Radio is fundamental to spaceflight because it's the one thing that reliably crosses hundreds of millions of kilometers of vacuum. But distance is brutal: received power falls off with the square of distance, so a signal from Mars can arrive fantastically weaker than one from a nearby cell tower. Engineers fight this with transmit power, antenna gain and pointing, receiver sensitivity, careful coding, and patience — the sum of all of it is called a link budget: a running tally of every gain and loss between transmitter and receiver, checked to make sure what's left over is still readable above the noise[3].

Doppler shift

A spacecraft moving toward you shifts its signal to a slightly higher frequency; moving away, slightly lower — the same effect as a passing ambulance siren, just applied to radio. It's not interference, it's a direct readout of relative velocity, and it matters for tracking satellites, deep-space navigation, radar, and amateur satellite work alike.

08 — Telemetry & Telecommand, Everywhere

Telemetry Is Not a Space Technology

"Telemetry" just means measuring something remotely and sending the measurement elsewhere. Spacecraft made the word famous; almost everything with a sensor and a radio does it now.

Telemetry and telecommand are the two halves of remote operation, and they say opposite things:

Telemetry

"Here is what I am measuring."

Telecommand

"Do this."

Telecommand shows up everywhere alongside telemetry — spacecraft, aircraft, drones, industrial equipment, remote sensors, robotics, satellites. Because a wrong or spoofed "do this" can be dangerous, safety-critical systems generally wrap commands in multiple layers: authentication, validation, redundancy, and fault handling, so a single corrupted or malicious message can't simply be obeyed.

09 — Military & Specialized Communications

Conceptual Only — No Operational Detail Here

Militaries are major users of the radio spectrum, across essentially every band discussed so far — HF, VHF, UHF, SHF, microwave links, satellite communications, and radar. Their use spans tactical communications, command and control, satellite links, radar, navigation, identification, electronic warfare, data links, and aircraft and maritime communications.

What tends to distinguish military systems conceptually, rather than any specific frequency, is an emphasis on reliability, mobility, anti-jamming techniques, secure communications, frequency agility (fast-switching or spread-spectrum methods that resist jamming and interception), network resilience, sometimes low probability of detection or interception, and built-in redundancy. This page stays at that conceptual level deliberately — no operational frequencies, classified details, or techniques for defeating communications belong here, or anywhere educational.

11 — Who Decides Who Uses Which Frequency?

Spectrum Is Managed, Not Owned

Frequency allocation isn't "someone owns 144 MHz." The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) maintains a global Table of Frequency Allocations, splitting the usable spectrum (8.3 kHz up to hundreds of GHz) among more than forty defined types of radio service, region by region[4]. National regulators (the FCC in the US, Ofcom in the UK, and equivalents elsewhere) then implement and license those allocations domestically, sometimes adding their own restrictions.

Within a shared band, services can hold primary status (protected from interference) or secondary status (must not interfere with primary users, and gets no protection in return) — amateur radio, for instance, is often secondary in bands it shares with other services[4]. Allocations differ by country and region, which is why a frequency legal to transmit on in one place may be restricted or reserved elsewhere.

12 — Amateur Radio: the Public Window Into RF

One Small, Legally Accessible Slice

A licensed amateur ("ham") radio operator can legally transmit within specific bands set aside for the amateur service, under the rules of their license and jurisdiction. It's the most direct, hands-on way for an individual to actually go operate — not just observe — the radio spectrum: voice, Morse code, digital modes, satellites, APRS position/data reporting, packet radio, Earth-Moon-Earth (bouncing signals off the Moon), amateur television, emergency communication, and open-ended antenna and propagation experimentation[5].

Important Listening ≠ transmitting. In many places, simply receiving radio signals is broadly permitted, but transmitting is regulated and usually requires a license — rules vary sharply by country and by frequency/service. Always verify the applicable regulations before transmitting.

15 — The Core Idea

Information Rides on Waves

A radio wave is a physical phenomenon. A bit is an abstraction. A radio system is the engineering that connects the two: turn information into a physical signal, send it through an imperfect environment, recover it at the other end, and work out whether what arrived is actually correct.

That single problem, solved with different frequencies, different modulation, different antennas, different protocols, different power levels, and different regulations, is what quietly connects a person to another person, a phone to a base station, a sensor to a gateway, an aircraft to air traffic infrastructure, a satellite to Earth, and a spacecraft to a dish on the other side of the solar system.

Every one of them is really answering the same question: how do we move information through physical space?
