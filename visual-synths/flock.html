Flock

No bird in this flock knows the shape it is part of. Each one follows three local rules: keep your distance, match your neighbours' heading, stay close to them. The swirling form comes out of those rules alone. Watch it as starlings over a reed bed or sardines in open water, then take the picture apart: switch to Vectors to see the three rules as coloured forces on every bird. You can hunt the flock, lure it, drop objects in its way, or change the rules while it flies.

How it works

Three rules, no leader. Every frame, each boid looks only at the neighbours it can perceive and turns according to three steering rules. Separation pushes it away from neighbours that are too close (inside half the perception radius, harder the closer they are). Alignment turns its velocity towards the average velocity of its neighbours. Cohesion pulls it towards their average position. Each rule gives an acceleration, the three are weighted by the sliders, summed and capped at the turn force. Speed stays between a floor and a ceiling, because a starling or a sardine can't hover. Nothing anywhere stores the shape of the flock. It exists only as the result of thousands of these local decisions, which is what Craig Reynolds set out to show in 1987.

What a boid can see. Perception radius sets how far it looks. Field of view takes out a blind spot behind it, so the rules are not symmetric: a bird reacts to those ahead of it more than those behind. Everyone in range is Reynolds' metric rule: every neighbour within the radius counts. Nearest k is the topological rule found in real starling flocks by Ballerini and colleagues (2008): each bird tracks a fixed number of nearest neighbours, about seven, however tightly or loosely the flock is packed. That is why a real murmuration stays connected while it stretches and thins. In this study the k nearest are still taken only from within the perception radius.

Everything else. The flock flies inside a soft ellipsoid of air (or water); the closer it gets to the edge, the harder it is turned back, so it swoops instead of hitting a wall. Objects push boids away and sideways, so they flow round them, and no boid can enter one. The predator is faster than the flock but turns worse. Boids within its reach flee, and they raise an alarm that passes from neighbour to neighbour with a short lag. The alarmed birds roll away, and the roll shows as a dark band running through the flock: a simple model of the agitation waves filmed in real starling flocks under attack. Informed leaders are the few who know where to go: they steer towards a goal that switches ends every few seconds, and the rest follow them without knowing they exist (Couzin and colleagues, 2005). Centre lure is a fixed point that pulls the flock in, the way a bait ball holds a school. Keep level damps vertical motion, as fish hold depth.

The numbers in the corner. Polarisation is the length of the average heading: 1 means every boid points the same way, near 0 means they point every way. Milling is the average of each boid's angular momentum about the flock's centre, from the position and heading unit vectors: near 1 means they all circle the centre the same way. Together they tell apart the four presets, which match the collective states that Couzin and colleagues (2002) described: Swarm (both low), Mill (high milling), Stream (high polarisation) and Murmuration, which keeps switching between the two as the flock turns.

Layers. Natural draws each boid as a bird with flapping wings or a fish with a beating tail, banked into its turns, in its own world. Vectors darkens that world and draws the three rules as coloured forces (a sample of boids, or every boid when you pick a single rule), plus one focus boid in large: its perception radius, its blind spot, its personal space and lines to its neighbours. With Natural off you get the bare diagram on black: the world's box and every boid as a heading. Neighbours draws who listens to whom (up to eight links per boid, for a sample of boids). Trails draws where 420 of them have just been.

Limits

  • This is a steering model for pictures, not a fitted biological model. The rule weights are chosen by eye; nothing here is measured from real birds or fish, and the presets are tuned to land in each state, not to predict when real animals do.
  • The four states show up in the polarisation and milling readouts, but they are not guaranteed. Murmuration and Stream keep changing as the flock turns, and Mill needs the centre lure: without it the school slips into a stream within a few turns.
  • Wingbeats and tail beats are slowed so they read on screen (a real starling flaps about ten to fifteen times a second). Bodies are low-polygon shapes and are not to scale with the flock's spacing.
  • The simulation runs on the CPU with a uniform grid for neighbour search. On a slow device the study lowers the count by itself until you set Count by hand. With reduced motion set in your system, everything runs at 40 % speed.
