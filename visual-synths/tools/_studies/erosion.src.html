Erosion

A city block as it came out of a photogrammetry solve: 225,000 coloured points, with the holes, smears and floating specks that every scan has. Those flaws are kept. Then the block comes apart. A release front moves through it, points let go and fall, and they settle at the ground as a spreading pool, as if the stone had turned liquid. After a pause it all flies back up and the block stands again. Drag to walk around it.

How it works

Every point knows when it lets go. Each point gets a release time between 0 and 1. Front sweeps across the block along a direction that turns a little every cycle. From the top releases the highest points first. Random lets go anywhere. Signal shapes the front with a hidden sum of slow waves, so the block erodes in bays and headlands you never see the cause of. A little noise is added to every release time so no front is perfectly straight.

Falling and pooling. Once released, a point falls under gravity, y = y₀ − ½·g·τ², and keeps its colour. When it reaches the ground it doesn't stop dead: it slides outward from the block, further the higher it fell, as Flow at the ground allows, and settles into a thin layer. That makes a pool instead of a pile. Rebuilding plays the same motion backwards. All of this is worked out in the vertex shader from the time alone, so 225,000 points cost no more than drawing them.

The scan. The block is the same reconstruction used in the Photogrammetry Scan study: points from a Structure-from-Motion solve (AliceVision/Meshroom), stored as 16-bit positions and 8-bit colours in a 2 MB file. "Up" is the average up direction of the 251 solved cameras; the ground is set just below the lowest two percent of points. Nothing is cleaned: smears along the camera paths and stray points in the air fall with everything else.

Lineage

A study in the language of scan-based work that treats a survey as material rather than as a record: imperfect captures kept as they are, and a process (erosion, melting) acting on them. It recreates no specific work.

Limits

  • There is no collision between points: the pool is a rule (slide outward, settle low), not a fluid simulation.
  • The scan's source is unknown. It is a sample reconstruction whose file names suggest rendered aerial footage, so it is not presented as a survey of a real, named place.
  • On phones half of the points are shown by default; raise Points shown if your device keeps up.
