Video Synth · Instrument · Map projections

Rind

The Earth is round and paper is flat. Every map has to stretch the globe to lay it flat, and every map stretches it in a different way. Choose a projection (a rule for flattening the globe), move its centre anywhere on Earth, and drag the land through it. The colours and circles stay with the rule; the land moves through them. The globe beside the map shows where the map is true and where it is cut.

Drag the map to move the land through the projection. Drag the white ring on the globe to move the centre; drag anywhere else on the globe to look around. Shift-drag or two-finger twist turns the heading; the wheel or a pinch zooms; double-click puts the centre there. How it works

From globe to paper, backwards. A projection is a formula that turns latitude and longitude into x and y. The graphics card runs it backwards for every pixel: "which place on Earth lands here?" (the inverse projection), then colours that place from a map of the coastline. The coastline is stored as a distance field, so its edge stays crisp even where a projection stretches it (the detail is still that of a 1:50 million map).

Moving the centre. Before projecting, the whole sphere is turned so that the chosen centre sits where the formula is at its best: this is called an oblique aspect. Turning a sphere stretches nothing, so the kind of distortion stays exactly the same; only its place on Earth moves. That is why the heat map and the lines of no distortion stay put on the screen while you drag the land through them. Dragging is a trackball: the place you grabbed stays under your finger.

Measuring the stretch. At every point the page computes how the map stretches a tiny step in each direction (the local scale matrix). Its largest and smallest stretch, a and b, are the axes of Tissot's ellipse. Area changes by a × b; the largest angle bend is 2ω, where sin ω = (a − b) / (a + b). Conformal maps keep a = b (shapes kept, areas change); equal-area maps keep a × b = 1 (areas kept, shapes change). It follows from Gauss's 1827 theorem that no flat map can do both: a sphere's curvature cannot be flattened without stretching, the way an orange peel tears when pressed flat.

Tissot circles. Each circle is the true image of a circle of radius 6° (or 3°) on the globe, computed from the angle between every pixel's place and the circle's centre. Small circles turn into Tissot's ellipses; large ones show the curved distortion honestly, which is why some look egg-shaped near Mercator's edge.

Routes and areas. The great circle is the shortest path, drawn as the plane through the Earth's centre and both places. The rhumb line keeps one compass bearing; it is a straight line on Mercator. Lengths use a sphere of radius 6,371 km. A carried country is turned rigidly across the sphere and re-projected every frame; its area "as drawn" adds up the map's area scale over thousands of points inside it.

Lineage

An instrument in the language of the cartographer's drafting table and the museum globe: a fine grid, a single bright line where the paper touches the Earth, and a field of colour that shows what the flattening costs. It recreates no specific work.

Limits

  • The Earth is a perfect sphere here. Real mapping uses an ellipsoid, slightly flattened at the poles, which moves positions by up to about 20 km and changes the numbers slightly.
  • Some projections run to infinity (Mercator's poles, the far side of the stereographic and gnomonic maps). They are clipped: Mercator at 85.05°, stereographic at 160° and gnomonic at 70° from the centre; colours beyond ×8 are hatched as "off the scale".
  • Coastlines are Natural Earth at 1:50 million, so small islands are simplified. Carried countries use the same outlines; their computed true areas differ from published ones by a few percent.
  • Robinson is defined by a table of numbers; between the table's rows this page interpolates with a smooth cubic, which can differ from other software by about one part in ten thousand.
  • Real flight paths follow winds and air-traffic rules, so they are not exactly great circles.
  • The colour scales are chosen for common colour-vision differences and have Bands with isolines, so they can be read without colour; they have not yet been tested with visitors.
  • Nothing flashes: tour moves take 1.6 s and changes of projection fade over 0.6 s. With reduced motion nothing moves on its own.
