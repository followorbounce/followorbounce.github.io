Heavy Machinery: The Blueprint

Every machine page in the Heavy Machinery Encyclopedia already draws its subject next to a human figure, to scale — because a number like "800 tonnes" or "4,000 horsepower" means very little until it's measured against something the eye already understands. This page takes that idea and puts all 20 machines on one blueprint: ranked side by side by height, weight, engine power, and load capacity, with every figure translated into how many people that number actually represents.

Core idea: the machine is drawn once, at full scale. It's the human reference that shrinks or multiplies to show you what the number means.

01 — Reading The Blueprint

Why Compare By Human Scale

Raw specifications don't stack up against each other on their own terms. A bucket-wheel excavator's 14,200 tonnes and a motor grader's 65.8 tonnes are both just "weight" until you have a common unit to convert them into — and the most legible common unit anyone carries around is their own body.

So every number on this page gets two treatments: a ranked bar against the other 19 machines (on a log scale, since the range between the smallest and largest machine here spans several orders of magnitude), and a human-equivalent count — how many average adults it would take, stacked, weighed, or powered, to equal that one machine. The pictogram rows make that count visible directly: each small figure represents a fixed number of people, shared across every machine in that category, so the length of the row of figures is itself a comparison.

04 — Methodology

What The Human-Equivalent Numbers Actually Mean

These conversions are illustrative, not scientific claims — they exist to make magnitude legible, not to model how 200,000 people would actually be arranged next to a bucket-wheel excavator.

Reference body mass70 kg
Reference body volume0.075 m³
Sustained human power output0.1 hp (~75 W)

Weight converts at 70 kg per person. Capacity converts at 0.075 m³ per person (a rough packing volume for an average adult body, not a claim about how people would actually fit). Power converts against 0.1 hp — roughly the power a person can put out continuously, not a short sprint or lift, which can be several times higher.

Height figures and their human-multiples are taken directly from each machine's own scale-comparison diagram in the Heavy Machinery Encyclopedia, and mix genuinely different reference points by necessity: some are the machine's transport or operating height, others are a crane's maximum boom height or a drill's mast height at full extension — the individual machine page always specifies which.

Weight only includes machines whose own page states the machine's own operating weight as a distinct figure — not a payload, lift capacity, or load it carries. That excludes machines like the Caterpillar 797F (a payload rating, not the truck's own weight) and the Sarens SGC-250 (a 5,000 t lift capacity, not the crane's own mass).

Power only includes a stated engine or installed power figure. The Spartacus dredger's 44,180 kW installed electrical power is converted to horsepower (×1.341) for comparability with the diesel-rated machines; everything else already uses each page's own hp figure.

Capacity groups every machine whose page states a bucket, blade, dipper, or tank capacity as a single volume figure, converting litres and cubic yards to cubic metres. These are physically different kinds of containers (an excavator dipper vs. a combine's grain tank vs. a dozer blade) grouped only because they share a unit — the full table above lists which is which.

Full specifications, sourcing, and engineering context for each machine live on its own page in the Heavy Machinery Encyclopedia.
